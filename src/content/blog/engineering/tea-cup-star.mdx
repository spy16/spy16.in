We ordered masala dose and tea for breakfast (sorry if I'm making you jealous). The hot tea comes in a round plastic container with a snap-on lid. Oddly, every time we order, the container arrives deformed into a sort of star shape.

I got curious, started searching and asked AI to explain. Apparently it's a phenomenon called **circumferential shell buckling**, and the star is a Fourier mode. Here's the whole story.

## Where the squeeze comes from

The tea is packed close to boiling, and the lid traps a pocket of air above it. At 85 °C that pocket is more than half steam: water vapour alone pushes with about 58 kPa, over half an atmosphere.

Then the tea cools. The steam condenses back into water and the remaining air contracts. By 50 °C the pressure inside has fallen to about half an atmosphere. The air outside now pushes on the wall about 50 kPa harder than the air inside, roughly 5 tonnes per square metre, on a wall under half a millimetre thick, made of plastic the tea has just softened.

Hot-filled juice bottles have exactly this problem. Many of them have flat "vacuum panels" moulded into their sides so they can cave in politely instead of randomly.

## Why a star, and not a crumple

A thin cylinder squeezed from outside doesn't shrink evenly. It snaps into a wavy shape, and any wavy dent around the circumference can be written as a sum of Fourier modes:

```
w(θ) = Σ Aₙ · cos(nθ)
```

Mode `n` puts `n` lobes around the cup: `n = 2` is an oval, `n = 3` a rounded triangle, and so on. Each mode needs a different pressure to get going, and whichever mode gives up first wins. Two costs compete:

- **Few, wide lobes** have to stretch the wall along its height, because the base and the lid hold the top and bottom in place. Stretching a wall is expensive.
- **Many, narrow lobes** have to bend the wall into tight curves, and bending gets expensive fast as `n` grows.

The cheapest compromise sits somewhere in the middle. For a cup of radius `R`, height `L` and wall thickness `t`, it works out to roughly

```
n ≈ 2.74 · √(R/L) · (R/t)^¼
```

which for a typical tea cup is about 7 or 8 lobes. A star.

A taller cup makes fewer, longer lobes. A thinner wall makes more of them, and gives up at a much lower pressure. With any realistic wall, the tea only has to cool a few degrees before the cup loses.

## What I'm hand-waving

- The formula is for a perfect cylinder. Real cups are tapered, have a stiff rim and a moulded base, and tiny dents help buckling start even earlier.
- Once the wall caves in, the volume inside shrinks and relieves part of the vacuum, so the dents stop at a finite depth.
- Hot plastic creeps, so some of the star can stay even after you pop the lid open.
